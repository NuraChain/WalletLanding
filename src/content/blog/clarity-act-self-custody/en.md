On 15 September 2026 the US Senate voted on whether to take up the CLARITY Act, the bill meant to set out how digital-asset markets are regulated in the United States. Moving forward needed 60 votes. It fell short, and the bill did not advance. A motion to reconsider was entered, which keeps a procedural route open; as of 4 October no new vote had been scheduled, according to the tracker kept by DeFi Rate.

Coverage called it a setback for the industry. Whether it is a setback for you depends on what you thought the bill was for.

## What is the CLARITY Act?

A market-structure bill. Its main work is deciding which regulator oversees which assets, and what exchanges, brokers and custodians have to do. The Senate draft also carries the Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act — protections for developers who write software without controlling anyone's funds — and directions for rules on DeFi trading protocols that somebody does control.

## Does the failed vote affect self-custody wallets?

Not directly. Holding your own keys did not depend on this bill passing, and it is not withdrawn by the bill stalling. A self-custodial wallet is software that stores a secret on your device and signs with it. No part of that runs through a registered intermediary, which is why most of a market-structure bill does not reach it.

What the bill would add is certainty for the people who write such software: a statute saying that publishing a wallet is not the same as running a financial business. Without it, that position rests on existing practice and guidance, which is less than a law and more than nothing.

## What should a wallet user take from it?

- The rules that touch you most are at the edges: the exchange where you buy, and what it must ask when you withdraw.
- Nothing about a stalled bill requires you to move, register or verify anything. Messages saying otherwise are [the usual thing](/blog/wallet-drainers/).
- [Self-custody is a technical fact first](/blog/self-custody-explained/): who holds the key. Law decides what happens around that fact.
- [Whether you can read a wallet's code](/blog/choosing-a-wallet/) matters more while the law is unsettled, because the code is the part that does not wait on a vote.

Nura is MIT-licensed software with no account and no server holding your phrase. That describes how it is built, not what any law says about it — and where you live may ask things of you that a wallet cannot know.
